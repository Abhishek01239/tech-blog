# Chip Stocks Tumble as Wall Street Asks: Is the AI Boom a Bubble?

For months, the artificial intelligence trade seemed unstoppable. Giant chipmakers, cloud providers, and data-center builders lifted Wall Street to one record after another. This week, the music stopped — at least temporarily — as a brutal sell-off swept through the technology sector and forced investors to confront a question they had been happy to ignore: what happens if the most expensive technology bet in history stops paying off?

## A Week of Red Numbers

The damage was fast and broad. The tech-heavy Nasdaq composite posted its first weekly loss in five weeks, and the S&P 500 logged its worst week since June. Chip stocks — the engine of the AI boom — took the hardest hits. Micron Technology fell more than five percent, Advanced Micro Devices dropped over five percent, Broadcom slid more than four percent, and Intel lost close to six percent on Friday alone. AI data-center infrastructure company CoreWeave, a major Nvidia customer, plunged more than seven percent after a double-digit decline earlier in the week.

The rout was global. Seoul's tech-rich Kospi briefly triggered a trading halt after diving more than eight percent, with Samsung sinking over nine percent and SK hynix down more than eleven percent. Tokyo shed close to five percent, dragged down by chip-linked names like Advantest and Kioxia as well as investment giant SoftBank.

## Why Now? Inflation and the Cost of Money

The immediate trigger was economic, not technological. US inflation data came in hotter than expected, with the consumer price index jumping to 3.7 percent in September and wholesale inflation surging 6.3 percent year-on-year — its biggest jump since 2022. Those numbers stoked fears that the Federal Reserve, which raised rates in September for the first time in three years, may have to tighten further, with futures markets now pricing in a high probability of another hike by December.

Higher rates matter more to AI companies than to almost anyone else. Tech giants have financed an unprecedented infrastructure spree — data centers, GPU clusters, and power-hungry compute farms — largely through debt markets. With bond yields climbing to their highest levels in years, the cost of financing that buildout is rising exactly as patience with unprofitable AI ventures is wearing thin. A week before the crash, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh had called AI "the most consequential change" to the global economy in his lifetime. Investors this week appeared to decide that consequence cuts both ways.

## The IMF's Warning Shot

The market turmoil coincided with an uncomfortable warning from the International Monetary Fund. At the IMF-World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok, the fund released a report projecting that a decline in AI investment could reduce global GDP by 0.4 percent by 2028, and cautioned that "weaker or less durable AI investments and profitability could be a shock that triggers a market correction."

IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva did not mince words: if confidence evaporates and "money runs away from AI, that can lead to financial stability risks" that "would be felt across the world." Yet the same meetings underscored how central AI already is to the global economy. The fund noted that global trade grew 8.3 percent between 2023 and 2025 — a figure that would have been closer to 4.6 percent without AI-related products. Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem put it bluntly: "It is here, and it's just the beginning."

## Bubble or Growing Pains?

Not everyone reading the sell-off sees a bubble bursting. The Dow Jones actually rose on Friday, suggesting investors are rotating out of tech rather than abandoning equities altogether. Earnings expectations for the quarter remain strong, and companies at the heart of the AI supply chain — from TSMC to memory makers — have continued to report robust demand. Nvidia shares, though down more than five percent for the week, remain up dramatically over the longer arc of the AI era.

The bear case, though, is no longer fringe. Critics point to debt-financed capital expenditure, stretched valuations, and monetization that still lags the hype. Analysts this week cited "worries about the AI buildout, bubbly valuations, lower-than-expected earnings growth at leading US frontier labs, and Chinese competition" as compounding pressures. Moody's Analytics economists added that a prolonged Middle East conflict, by pushing up energy prices and interest rates, could "puncture" the AI boom by straining the economics behind AI investment.

## What to Watch

Three signposts will matter in the coming weeks. First, the Fed's October 28 policy decision, landing a week before the US midterm elections — a hawkish surprise could renew the pressure on rate-sensitive tech names. Second, the ongoing earnings season, where markets will demand evidence that AI revenue is catching up to AI spending. Third, and hardest to measure: confidence. As Georgieva noted in Bangkok, "if there are no safeguards, then the risks may exceed the opportunities."

Whether this week proves to be a healthy correction in a long structural boom or the first crack in a bubble, one thing is clear: the era of unquestioned AI enthusiasm on Wall Street is over. From now on, the market will want receipts.
