SpaceX announced on October 8, 2026, a definitive agreement to acquire 100% of Grain Management’s nationwide 800 MHz low-band spectrum portfolio. The deal, subject to FCC approval, is intended to supply the coverage layer that lets Starlink Mobile deliver service indoors on ordinary smartphones and compete as a facilities-based US mobile carrier.

## What Happened

Grain Management, a digital-infrastructure investment firm, confirmed the agreement in a press release the same day. SpaceX will take the entire portfolio—up to 14 MHz of paired spectrum in the 817–824 MHz and 862–869 MHz bands. The licenses cover approximately 100 percent of the US population, though the exact bandwidth varies by county from about 4.85 MHz to the full 14 MHz.

SpaceX said the low-band assets address “one of the key remaining technical gaps” for Starlink Mobile. Its existing 2 GHz mid-band spectrum (acquired from EchoStar in a $17 billion deal approved by the FCC in May 2026) supplies capacity outdoors and via satellite. The 800 MHz band adds building penetration so signals can reach devices inside walls. Most current smartphones already support the frequencies, so no special handsets are required.

Elon Musk called the transaction a “very big deal” on X. SpaceX posted that the combination of the new spectrum and its Gen2 constellation will let Starlink Mobile provide high-speed mobile broadband “no matter where they are.”

## Details

The spectrum originated with T-Mobile (from its 2020 Sprint acquisition). A Department of Justice merger condition required T-Mobile to divest it. Dish had a first-refusal option that expired; Grain bought the portfolio from T-Mobile in August 2026 in a cash-and-spectrum swap valued around $2.9 billion. Grain then ran a process to place the underutilized licenses with an operator that could put them to productive use. The FCC’s July 2026 order approving Grain’s purchase explicitly allowed terrestrial, satellite-to-mobile, or hybrid use and set a competitive selection path.

SpaceX has filed with the FCC for approval of the transfer and has described a terrestrial deployment of sectorized antennas on towers, rooftops, and other structures to use the 800 MHz layer. It pairs this with the satellite network. Earlier in the same week the FCC authorized SpaceX to deploy up to 15,000 next-generation direct-to-device satellites that it says will deliver more than 100 times the bandwidth of the current generation and will operate with its existing gateway network.

Financial terms of the SpaceX–Grain deal were not disclosed. Some reports cited an approximately $8 billion figure; neither party confirmed a price. Closing remains contingent on FCC approval and customary conditions.

## Why It Matters

Low-band spectrum is scarce and especially valuable for coverage. Satellite-only direct-to-device service has historically struggled with indoor reliability. By owning a nationwide low-band coverage layer in addition to mid-band capacity and a large satellite constellation, SpaceX can design a hybrid network that aims to work indoors, outdoors, and in cellular dead zones on unmodified phones. That shifts Starlink Mobile from a wholesale coverage partner (it currently works with T-Mobile in the US) toward a potential retail competitor to AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile.

Carrier stocks dropped sharply on the news. Analysts noted that while SpaceX still holds far less total spectrum than the incumbents (roughly 79 MHz of low- and mid-band versus several hundred MHz each for the big three), the combination of orbital and terrestrial assets removes a key technical objection to a standalone service.

## Context

SpaceX has spent years assembling spectrum. The EchoStar mid-band purchase and the new low-band agreement, plus the Gen2 satellite authorization, form the latest pieces. Musk has previously said the company would rely more on many small base stations than on large traditional towers, and that existing Starlink roof antennas could assist cellular connectivity. The big three carriers recently formed a joint venture to pool spectrum for satellite dead-zone coverage, signaling they are preparing for more satellite competition.

Rural wireless groups have previously raised concerns about spectrum aggregation when EchoStar licenses moved. Opposition to the present deal is possible but not yet dominant in public filings.

## Impact

For consumers, the practical timeline depends on FCC approval, terrestrial build-out, and satellite launches. SpaceX has indicated next-generation direct-to-device service could begin in late 2027 in some markets, with thousands more satellites by the end of 2028. Until then, the current T-Mobile partnership and limited direct-to-cell service continue. Verizon noted that spectrum alone is not a network; infrastructure still has to be built.

Investors treated the announcement as a material competitive threat to traditional carriers. For SpaceX, successful approval would give it the spectrum foundation to offer a bundled broadband-plus-mobile service without relying solely on partner spectrum.

## What’s Next

The FCC must review the transfer. SpaceX will continue Gen2 satellite deployment and refine its hybrid architecture. Grain completes its role as an intermediary that moved underutilized spectrum to a new use case. Expect further detail on terrestrial density, pricing, and device compatibility as regulatory milestones clear.

## TechPulse Takeaway

The October 8 agreement is a confirmed step that supplies Starlink Mobile with a nationwide low-band coverage layer. Terms are undisclosed and FCC approval is still required, so the deal is not yet closed. If approved and executed, it meaningfully narrows the technical gap between a satellite-first service and a full mobile carrier offering on ordinary phones. The outcome will hinge as much on build-out speed and spectrum efficiency as on the headline acquisition.
